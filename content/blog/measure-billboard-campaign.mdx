To measure a billboard campaign, check two things separately: **delivery**, whether the advertising ran as booked and how many people passed it; and **response**, whether those people did something afterwards. Delivery is proved with the site's audience figure, a dated photograph of every face and a play log for every screen. Response is measured with tools the advertiser controls, such as a dedicated phone or WhatsApp number, an offer code, or a before-and-after comparison of enquiries, searches and footfall.

Billboards have no click, so measurement has to be planned before the campaign starts, not after.

## What can you measure about a billboard campaign?

| Question | What answers it |
|---|---|
| Did it run? | A dated photograph of every static face; a play log for every screen |
| How many people passed it? | The daily audience figure published for each site |
| Was it seen at night? | The face's illumination: front-lit, back-lit, digital or non-lit |
| Did people respond? | Enquiries, searches, visits and sales, tracked by the advertiser |

The first three are the media owner's job. The fourth is yours, and the next sections cover how.

## How are billboard impressions estimated?

A billboard's audience is the number of people who pass the face each day. Fangrove Media publishes that figure, called audience per day, for every site on the [network map](/network), alongside the face's size, lighting and direction of approach. A campaign plan multiplies those daily figures across the faces and days booked to give an impression model, and you should see the assumptions behind it, line by line.

Two cautions. Audience is the number of people who *pass* a face, not the number who read it. And a face is only seen by traffic driving towards it, which is why the direction of approach matters as much as the headline number.

## How do you measure response to a billboard?

Plan at least one of these before the board goes up:

1. **A dedicated number.** Put a phone or WhatsApp number on the board that appears nowhere else. Every call to it came from outdoor.
2. **An offer code or keyword.** "Mention BOULEVARD at the counter" or a short code for the website. Simple, and countable.
3. **A before-and-after baseline.** Record weekly enquiries, branded searches, store footfall or sales for several weeks before the campaign, then compare the same figures during and after it.
4. **Geography.** If the boards run in one area of the city, compare branches or postcodes inside that area with those outside it.
5. **Ask.** A "how did you hear about us?" question on forms and at the till still works.

Keep the response mechanism short. A long URL or a ten-digit code will not be remembered from a moving car.

## How long before you judge a billboard campaign?

Outdoor works through repetition, so give it time. Static faces are booked in monthly cycles, from the 1st or the 16th, and one cycle on one face rarely shows what the medium can do. Judge a campaign over its whole run, against the baseline, and allow for the delay between seeing a board and acting on it, which is longer for considered purchases such as cars, property or education.

## What does good reporting look like?

At the end of a campaign you should be able to answer, for every placement:

- Where it was, what size and how it was lit.
- When it went up and came down, with dated proof.
- The audience it carried, and the assumptions behind that figure.
- What it cost.

Put that alongside your own response figures and you can compare one corridor, format or medium with another, and plan the next campaign on evidence.
